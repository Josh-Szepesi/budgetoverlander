Waking up to water beading on the ceiling and running down the walls is the quickest way to learn that a camper van is a small, sealed, metal box. Every breath, every pot of boiling water, and every wet jacket adds vapor to the air, and the cold steel skin of the van is the first place that vapor turns back into liquid. Left alone, that moisture soaks insulation, stains plywood, and starts rust in places you will not see until the damage is done.

The good news is that you do not need a $2,000 climate system to fix this. For most budget builds, the winning order is airflow first, measurement second, and passive moisture absorbers third. A reversible roof fan and a couple of cracked windows do more than any gadget, a $15 hygrometer tells you whether your changes are working, and renewable desiccant covers the gaps. That combination fits comfortably under $500 even after you add the small parts people forget.

This guide walks through how moisture builds up, how to design an airflow path, which products earn a spot in a budget build, and the mistakes that bring condensation back.

## Why Camper Vans Collect Moisture So Quickly

Condensation is simple physics. Warm air holds more water vapor than cold air. When humid interior air touches a surface colder than its dew point, the vapor drops out as liquid. A van has a lot of those cold surfaces: bare steel ribs, roof panels, window frames, and the wheel wells. If you want a deeper look at keeping outside water out, the guide on [weatherproofing windows, doors, and seals](/2026/10/22/how-to-weatherproof-windows-doors-and-seals-against-moisture-and-dust-infiltrati/) covers the leak side of the problem. This article is about the moisture you generate yourself.

Commonly cited estimates put human respiration and perspiration at roughly a pint of water per person overnight, and two people sleeping in a closed van can push interior humidity high before sunrise. Cooking adds more. Burning propane produces water vapor as a combustion byproduct, and boiling pasta water without a vent turns the cabin into a sauna. Wet boots, damp towels, and a dog that swam in the creek all contribute too.

Three sources matter most, in this order:

- **Breathing and sleeping:** constant, unavoidable, and the main reason vans fog up overnight.
- **Cooking and hot water:** large bursts of vapor in a short time, which is why cooking ventilation matters more than people expect.
- **Wet gear and weather:** slow but cumulative, especially in cold, rainy trips.

The EPA's indoor air quality guidance recommends keeping indoor relative humidity roughly between 30 and 50 percent to limit mold growth, and that is a sensible target for a van too. In a space this small, you will not hit it by wishing. You will hit it by moving air out, measuring what remains, and absorbing the rest. You can read the agency's overview at the [EPA indoor air quality page](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq).

## DIY Ventilation Starts With an Airflow Path

Most beginners buy a fan and expect magic. A fan only works if air has somewhere to come from. Think of ventilation as a path with an intake, a route through the living space, and an exhaust. If any of the three is blocked, the system stalls and the moisture stays.

The simplest effective layout is cross-ventilation with a high exhaust and a low or distant intake. Warm, humid air rises, so the roof is the best place to pull it out. Fresh air should enter from the opposite end of the van, ideally from a cracked window or a small screened vent near the foot of the bed. A fan at the front with a window cracked at the rear makes the whole cabin flush instead of just the area under the fan.

> **Safety first.** If you cook with propane, run a heater, or sleep with any combustion appliance in the van, install a battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm and check it before every trip. Never run a fuel-burning heater or stove as your only source of warmth in a sealed van, and never rely on ventilation to make an unvented heater safe. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission publishes clear guidance in its [carbon monoxide information center](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center). If you are unsure about cutting a roof hole, wiring a fused 12V circuit, or routing a gas line, hire a professional installer.

Here is how the main options compare on a tight budget:

- **Cracked windows with rain guards:** the cheapest option. Window vent visors let you leave windows open in drizzle. They are weak on their own, but they are a perfect intake partner for a roof fan.
- **Passive roof vents:** a low-profile vent with no motor. These help a little, but without forced airflow they do not keep up with two sleepers and a stove.
- **Powered roof fan:** the best return on your money. It moves a measurable volume of air, runs on 12V, and can be reversed to pull in fresh air during hot afternoons.

Plan your power draw too. A roof fan on its lowest setting draws very little, but it runs for hours overnight. ## Moisture Control Inside the Van: Measure, Absorb, Insulate

Ventilation removes most of the water, but you still need to know what is left. A hygrometer turns guesswork into data. The [Govee H5075 Bluetooth hygrometer and thermometer](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07Y36FWTT?tag=budgetoverlander-20) tracks temperature and humidity on your phone, and the listing mentions exportable data storage of up to two years. Keep in mind it connects over Bluetooth, so it needs your phone nearby to sync and alert. That is fine for a van, where you are always within range.

Place the sensor away from the stove and away from direct sun, ideally at head height near the bed. Watch the overnight curve. If humidity climbs past about 60 percent while you sleep, your ventilation is not keeping up. If it spikes only when you cook, the answer is a cooking vent window or a pause in the fan routine.

For passive absorption, the [Eva-Dry E-500 renewable silica gel dehumidifier 5-pack](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08GY6XQ9H?tag=budgetoverlander-20) is a useful low-effort tool. The listing rates each unit for 500 cubic feet, says it needs no batteries, and describes plugging it in to renew. Set it honestly in your expectations: a single small desiccant canister will not rescue a van with no airflow. Use them in closed spaces where air stagnates, like storage lockers, under-bed bins, and gear cubbies. Those are the places where mildew shows up first, as covered in the guide to [DIY interior storage and cargo organization](/2026/10/20/diy-interior-storage-and-cargo-organization-for-overlanding-vehicles/).

Insulation matters too, because it keeps interior surfaces above the dew point. Closed-cell foam board or sheep wool outperforms fiberglass in a van because it does not hold water. Cover every steel gap, including ribs, with a thermal break such as thin foam tape before you panel over it. Fewer cold surfaces means less condensation to manage.

That adds up to roughly $340 to $540 depending on how many extras you choose, so trim the visors or tape if you are near the ceiling. Do not cut the sealant, the fused wiring, or the CO alarm.

Follow this order for a weekend install:

1. **Measure and mark the roof.** Confirm the opening between ribs, trace the fan flange, and check the underside for wiring and structural members before you cut.
2. **Cut and treat the opening.** Use a jigsaw or nibbler, file the edges smooth, and apply rust-inhibiting primer to bare metal immediately.
3. **Install the fan with sealant.** Bed the flange in roof-rated sealant, secure it per the manufacturer's instructions, and let the sealant cure before testing in rain.
4. **Wire a fused circuit.** Run power from the house battery through a properly sized fuse and a switch, and protect the wire where it passes through any metal edge.
5. **Add the intake.** Install window visors or a screened low vent opposite the fan so air can cross the whole cabin.
6. **Place the hygrometer and desiccants.** Put the sensor near the bed and the desiccant canisters in lockers and under-bed storage.
7. **Test overnight.** Sleep with the fan on low and review the humidity graph the next morning, then adjust fan speed and intake size.

## Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting Condensation Problems

Even a good setup fails when small details are wrong. These are the problems that show up most often in budget builds, and each one has a fix.

**Running the fan with every window sealed.** If air cannot enter, the fan strains and barely moves anything. Crack a window or open an intake vent on the opposite end of the van, then watch the hygrometer for a drop.

**Cooking without venting.** A single boiling pot can spike humidity in minutes. Open the fan on high and a window while you cook, and keep lids on pots when you can. This also helps with combustion byproducts from propane, which is another reason to keep that CO alarm working.

**Relying on desiccants alone.** Silica gel is a supplement, not a substitute for airflow. If the canisters fill up in a day, your real problem is a lack of ventilation.

**Skipping the rust treatment at the roof cut.** Raw steel edges around the fan opening can start rusting within a season if they are not sealed. Prime the edges, seal the flange, and inspect it every few months. The same principle applies underneath the vehicle, as the [undercarriage rust repair guide](/2026/08/16/how-to-seal-and-repair-undercarriage-rust-on-an-overland-vehicle/) explains.

**Ignoring cold bridges.** If you only see condensation along specific lines, those are likely uninsulated ribs or window frames. Add thermal break tape or foam to those spots.

**Drying wet gear inside overnight.** Hanging soaked clothes in the cabin adds liters of vapor. Dry them in the sun, in a ventilated vestibule, or under an awning when possible.

**Never checking the numbers.** If you do not look at the hygrometer, you will not know what is working. Review the overnight graph after the first few trips and adjust your fan speed, intake size, and cooking habits accordingly.

## FAQ

### How much airflow does a camper van actually need?

There is no single number that fits every van, since occupancy, cooking, and climate all change the load. A practical rule is to aim for steady cross-ventilation overnight and a stronger burst while cooking. Start with the fan on low, a window cracked on the opposite side, and a hygrometer in place. If relative humidity stays near the 30 to 50 percent range recommended for indoor spaces, your setup is working. If it climbs above 60 percent, increase the fan speed or enlarge the intake before buying anything else.

### Can I use only desiccant packs and skip a fan?

You can, but it is a weak strategy for anyone sleeping in the van. Silica gel absorbs a finite amount of water before it needs to be dried, and a sleeping person adds more vapor each night than a small canister can hold. Desiccants work best in closed spaces like storage lockers and gear bins. For living space, rely on moving air out of the van and use absorbers as a backup that protects stored items, bedding, and electronics.

### Is a roof fan worth the cost on a tight budget?

For most people, yes. It is the one purchase that addresses the root cause, which is trapped humid air, instead of treating symptoms. If your budget cannot stretch to a fan yet, begin with window rain guards, a low intake, and a pair of small 12V fans, then upgrade later. Just avoid spending money on multiple small gadgets that do not move air, since those add cost without lowering the humidity you actually breathe overnight.

### How do I know if my condensation problem is a leak or just humidity?

Check the pattern. Condensation tends to appear on cold surfaces across the whole cabin, usually at night or in the morning, and it eases once the air warms or dries. A leak leaves water in a specific spot, often after rain or a wash, and it keeps showing up regardless of occupancy. Run a hose test while someone watches inside, and inspect window seals, roof seams, and the fan flange. A hygrometer helps separate the two because a leak does not follow your breathing and cooking patterns.

## Conclusion

Controlling moisture in a budget camper van comes down to three habits: move air out, measure what remains, and protect the places air cannot reach. A reversible roof fan with a deliberate intake solves most condensation, a cheap hygrometer shows whether your fixes are working, and renewable desiccant keeps closed lockers from turning musty. The whole system fits under $500 as long as you do not skip the sealant, the fused wiring, or the carbon monoxide alarm.

Your next step is simple. Measure the roof space where a fan could go, buy the hygrometer first, and spend one week tracking your overnight humidity before you cut anything. That data will tell you exactly how much ventilation your build needs.
